Abstract
Text-to-SQL translation systems, which convert natural language questions into database
queries, are crucial for making data accessible. However, they still struggle with reliably
generating accurate SQL, especially when questions are phrased differently. This study
introduces an advanced Text-to-SQL framework that significantly boosts query accuracy
through an innovative, automated error repair mechanism.
Our proposed system features a robust six-layer architecture: it processes user input,
intelligently crafts prompts, uses a Large Language Model (LLM) (specifically GPT-4o) to
generate SQL queries, executes these queries on a database, and importantly, incorporates
a validation and correction layer. This correction layer automatically detects errors and
iteratively refines the generated SQL. Finally, it delivers the accurate results to the user.
We rigorously evaluated the framework on the Spider 1.0 dataset, a leading benchmark
with 1,034 diverse questions across 20 databases. Our experiments included testing various
LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o Mini, and Qwen-Coder) and revealed a critical insight: when
questions were rephrased into an "improved" format, accuracy *decreased* by 1.9-2.7
percentage points across all models. This unexpected finding highlights the systems’
sensitivity to subtle linguistic variations, suggesting a need for more robust semantic
understanding beyond simple phrasing.
Figure 1: Comparison of Original vs. Improved Question Accuracy Across LLMs with
and without Repair

The core contribution of this work is our sophisticated, intelligent repair mechanism.
This mechanism doesn’t just catch errors; it analyzes query execution failures and result
discrepancies. It then collaborates with the LLM through a feedback loop, employing
question intent analysis, schema verification, and up to three iterative refinement attempts
to generate a corrected SQL query. This approach aligns with concepts from papers
like MapleRepair (Shen et al., 2025) and DAC (Wang et al., 2024), which emphasize
understanding and correcting Text-to-SQL errors.
The complete system, powered by GPT-4o with the integrated repair mechanism, achieved
a remarkable 83.8% exact match accuracyon the Spider development set. This
represents a substantial **20.8 percentage point improvement** compared to a baseline
without the repair system (63.0%). This performance significantly surpasses previous
state-of-the-art results on Spider, such as STRUG’s 70.5% (Deng et al., 2021). The
repair mechanism proved highly effective, successfully correcting 49.4% of the queries it
attempted to fix.
Figure 2: Repair Mechanism Success Rate for GPT-4o
These compelling results unequivocally demonstrate that integrating an automated,
intelligent repair mechanism dramatically enhances the robustness and reliability of Text-
to-SQL systems. This makes them far more practical and dependable for real-world
applications where user questions are diverse and precise query execution is paramount.

Chapter 1
Introduction
This chapter establishes the research context by identifying critical limitations in current
text-to-SQL systems and presents our approach to addressing these challenges through
automated repair mechanisms. We outline the research questions, hypotheses, and
contributions that guide this investigation into developing practical, cost-effective solutions
for democratizing database access.
1.1 Problem Statement
Text-to-SQL systems represent a transformative technology that converts natural language
questions into structured database queries, fundamentally democratizing data access for
non-technical users. These systems promise to eliminate the barrier between human
curiosity and data insights by allowing users to ask questions in their native language
rather than learning complex SQL syntax. For routine data retrieval tasks, these systems
offer particular value to small and medium-sized organizations, providing cost-effective
alternatives to training custom models or hiring specialized database personnel for simple
queries like finding basic business metrics or retrieving standard reports. Recent advances
in large language models and natural language processing have led to improved baseline
performance, and several research efforts have begun exploring error correction mechanisms.
Notable approaches include MapleRepair [1], which investigates in-context learning-based
error correction for text-to-SQL systems, and DAC [2], which explores decomposition-
based correction strategies. These pioneering works have demonstrated that automated
repair mechanisms can improve text-to-SQL accuracy and have established important
foundations for error correction in this domain. However, despite these advances, current
text-to-SQL systems still face significant limitations that restrict their practical deployment
in real-world environments. Current systems achieve only around 70-75% accuracy on
standard benchmarks like Spider [3], meaning that one in four queries will be incorrect,
2

which creates serious risks in business-critical environments where healthcare settings
might experience incorrect queries about patient data affecting treatment decisions,
or financial organizations might encounter wrong revenue calculations leading to poor
strategic decisions. These systems also demonstrate extreme sensitivity to question
phrasing, where our preliminary testing reveals that even small changes in wording
can reduce accuracy by 2-3%, such as asking “Show me total sales in January” versus
“What are January’s overall sales?” producing different results despite conveying identical
analytical intent, suggesting that current systems memorize specific linguistic patterns
rather than developing robust semantic understanding. While approaches like MapleRepair
and DAC have made important contributions to text-to-SQL error correction, existing
error recovery capabilities remain limited in their scope and systematic effectiveness,
often focusing on specific error types or correction strategies without demonstrating
comprehensive improvements across diverse error categories and model architectures,
with many existing approaches addressing syntax-level corrections but struggling with
deeper semantic misunderstandings, schema interpretation errors, or intent misalignment
issues that characterize the majority of text-to-SQL failures. Additionally, these systems
exhibit brittleness when database structures evolve, where small modifications to database
schemas, such as renaming tables or adding columns, can cause complete system failures,
making them unreliable for production environments where database structures naturally
change over time. These limitations collectively create a substantial gap between the
theoretical potential of text-to-SQL systems and their practical utility in real-world
applications, where while existing repair mechanisms have demonstrated valuable proof-of-
concept results, they have not yet achieved the systematic effectiveness and comprehensive
error coverage needed for reliable production deployment, which our research addresses by
building upon existing repair work to develop more comprehensive automated correction
mechanisms that demonstrate systematic accuracy improvements across multiple model
architectures while effectively handling diverse error categories that previous approaches
have not adequately addressed.
1.1.1 Critical Accuracy Deficiencies in Production Environments
The most fundamental limitation plaguing current text-to-SQL systems is their inadequate
accuracy performance, which renders them unsuitable for production deployment in
business-critical environments. Contemporary state-of-the-art systems achieve only 70-
75% accuracy on standardized benchmarks, with leading approaches like STRUG reaching
70.5% accuracy and DIN-SQL achieving 74.2% on the Spider benchmark [4, 3]. This
performance ceiling represents a critical failure threshold that fundamentally undermines
system reliability and user trust.
3

The implications of 25-30% error rates extend far beyond simple inconvenience. As
confirmed by our preliminary testing, this means that approximately one in four queries
will be incorrect, which is fundamentally unacceptable for business-critical applications
requiring reliable database access. In business environments where data-driven decisions
impact resource allocation, strategic planning, and operational efficiency, incorrect query
results can propagate through analytical workflows, leading to flawed insights, misallocated
budgets, and compromised competitive positioning.
Financial institutions relying on inaccurate revenue calculations face substantial opera-
tional risks when quarterly reporting contains systematic errors. Healthcare organizations
making patient care decisions based on incorrect statistical analyses could compromise
treatment protocols and resource allocation. Manufacturing companies optimizing produc-
tion schedules with flawed data queries risk inefficient operations, inventory imbalances,
and reduced competitive positioning in dynamic markets.
Recent comprehensive surveys of large language model-enhanced text-to-SQL systems
confirm that accuracy limitations persist across different architectural approaches, model
sizes, and training methodologies [5]. Even with advances in transformer architectures [6]
and few-shot learning capabilities demonstrated by models like GPT-4 [7], the fundamen-
tal accuracy ceiling remains a persistent challenge that prevents widespread enterprise
adoption.
The accuracy problem becomes particularly acute when considering the compound effects
of query chaining, where initial incorrect queries serve as inputs for subsequent analytical
operations. A single misgenerated SQL query can cascade through reporting pipelines,
dashboard systems, and automated decision-making processes, amplifying the impact of
individual errors and potentially compromising entire analytical workflows. This cascading
failure mode creates multiplicative risk factors that exponentially increase the potential
for systematic analytical errors.
1.1.2 Systematic Absence of Intelligent Error Recovery Mecha-
nisms
Perhaps more concerning than low accuracy rates is the complete absence of systematic
error recovery mechanisms in current text-to-SQL architectures. When contemporary
systems generate incorrect SQL queries, they typically exhibit a binary success-failure
paradigm with no attempt at intelligent error correction or iterative refinement. This
architectural limitation represents a fundamental design flaw that abandons users at
precisely the moment when intelligent assistance becomes most valuable.
Current systems simply fail without attempting repairs when queries are incorrect, forcing
4

users to manually debug and correct errors. This requirement completely defeats the
fundamental value proposition of natural language database interfaces, which promise to
eliminate technical barriers to data access. Users who encounter failed queries must either
possess advanced SQL debugging skills or abandon their analytical tasks entirely, creating
a fundamental accessibility barrier that undermines system utility.
While pioneering research efforts like MapleRepair have begun exploring in-context learning
approaches for text-to-SQL error correction [1], and DAC (Decompose-Analyze-Correct)
has investigated decomposition-based correction strategies [2], these approaches remain
severely limited in scope and effectiveness. Current error correction attempts focus
primarily on syntax-level fixes and fail to address deeper semantic misunderstandings,
schema interpretation errors, or intent misalignment issues that characterize the majority
of text-to-SQL failures.
The absence of comprehensive error recovery mechanisms forces users to invest significant
time in manual debugging processes that require substantial SQL expertise. This time
investment negates the productivity benefits that automated query generation promises to
deliver. Organizations seeking to democratize data access find themselves constrained by
the technical expertise requirements that text-to-SQL systems were designed to eliminate.
Furthermore, current systems provide no feedback mechanisms to help users understand
why queries failed or how to reformulate their questions for better results. This opacity
creates frustrating user experiences and prevents iterative refinement of both user query
formulation and system performance. The lack of explanatory error messages, suggested
corrections, or alternative query formulations represents a missed opportunity for collabo-
rative human-AI problem-solving that could substantially improve system utility and user
satisfaction.
1.1.3 Economic Inefficiencies and Computational Cost Barriers
The deployment of large language models for text-to-SQL tasks involves substantial
computational costs that create significant barriers to widespread adoption, particularly
for small to medium-sized organizations with limited technical budgets. Training custom
text-to-SQL models requires extensive computational resources, specialized hardware
infrastructure, and substantial time investments that many organizations cannot afford.
The cost implications extend beyond initial training to include inference costs, model host-
ing, and ongoing maintenance requirements that can quickly escalate beyond reasonable
budget constraints.
Current approaches often require extensive fine-tuning on domain-specific datasets to
achieve acceptable performance levels, multiplying training costs and creating additional
5

barriers for organizations with unique database schemas or specialized analytical require-
ments. The computational expense of running large language models for frequent database
queries can quickly become prohibitive, particularly for organizations with high-volume
analytical workloads or limited cloud computing budgets.
However, if text-to-SQL systems could achieve high accuracy rates for small to medium
complexitytasksthrougheffectiverepairmechanisms, organizationscouldrealizesignificant
cost savings compared to training custom models or employing specialized database
analysts. A reliable text-to-SQL system could handle routine analytical queries, generate
standard reports, and support basic data exploration tasks without requiring expensive
specialized personnel or extensive model training investments.
The potential economic benefits extend beyond direct cost savings to include increased
analytical productivity, reduced time-to-insight, and democratized data access across
organizational hierarchies. Non-technical staff could independently access data for routine
analytical tasks, freeing skilled database analysts to focus on complex strategic analyses
that require specialized expertise. This productivity multiplier effect could justify text-to-
SQL system investments even for organizations with modest analytical needs, creating
scalable pathways for data democratization across diverse organizational contexts.
For small to medium-sized enterprises, successful text-to-SQL implementation could
eliminate the need for dedicated database specialists for routine queries, reducing personnel
costs while maintaining analytical capabilities. The cost-effectiveness becomes particularly
compelling when considering the alternative of training multiple staff members in SQL
programming or hiring specialized consultants for routine data access tasks.

2.1.1 Early Approaches and Neural Network Advancements
The initial attempts at text-to-SQL translation relied primarily on rule-based systems

by comprehensive surveys [8, 9, 5], were often domain-specific and struggled with
scalability and linguistic variations. Early rule-based approaches required extensive
manual engineering of grammar rules and semantic mappings, limiting their applicability
across diverse database domains and question formulations.
The introduction of neural networks marked a significant paradigm shift, enabling data-
driven learning from examples rather than manual rule specification. The transformer
architecture [6], with its attention mechanisms, further enhanced text-to-SQL capabilities
by enabling better modeling of complex relationships between natural language questions

Recent developments have leveraged the impressive capabilities of large pre-trained lan-

STRUG [4]: This method, based on structure-grounded pre-training, achieved 70.5%

TabSD[11]: This approach addresses scalability in table question answering, demonstrat-

production-ready accuracy in cross-domain text-to-SQL translation tasks.

the binary success-failure paradigm identified in previous text-to-SQL research [5]. The
system addresses: (1) SQL execution errors including syntax violations and constraint
violations that prevent query execution, (2) Result mismatches where queries execute
successfully but produce incorrect output, (3) Schema violations involving non-existent
table or column references, and (4) Semantic logic errors such as incorrect aggregation or
missing operations.
This implementation directly addresses the schema linking challenges identified by Deng
et al. (2021) in STRUG [strug2021], where text-table alignment represents a critical
bottleneck in text-to-SQL accuracy. The error detection framework operates by first
attempting SQL execution, then checking for syntax errors if execution fails, followed by
schema validation, and finally result comparison when gold standard answers are available.

3.4 Discussion and Implications
3.4.1 Understanding the Question Improvement Paradox
The counterintuitive finding that improved questions resulted in decreased performance
across all models challenges assumptions about the relationship between question clarity
and LLM performance in text-to-SQL tasks. This phenomenon suggests several important
implications for system design and deployment.
The question improvement process may have inadvertently introduced linguistic complexity
or patterns less familiar to the models’ training data. While improved questions appear
clearer to human readers, they might contain syntactic patterns or vocabulary choices that
deviate from the text-to-SQL examples used in model training. Since LLMs rely heavily
on pattern matching and statistical associations learned from training data, questions
that deviate from familiar patterns may actually impair performance despite seeming
more explicit.
Another explanation relates to potential semantic ambiguity introduced through the
improvement process. Original Spider questions, despite appearing less clear, may have
been formulated to correspond more directly to database structures and SQL logic.
Question improvements aimed at natural language clarity might introduce multiple
interpretations or require more sophisticated reasoning to map to correct SQL operations.
This finding has significant implications for user interface design in database querying
systems, suggesting that more natural-sounding questions may not always produce better
results. System designers should consider training users on question formulation patterns
that align with model capabilities rather than assuming that clearer natural language
necessarily improves performance.
3.4.2 Repair Mechanism Contributions and Limitations
The repair mechanism demonstrates both significant potential and important limitations in
automated error correction for text-to-SQL systems. The ability to successfully correct 43-
49% of initially failed queries for strong models like GPT-4o represents substantial practical
value, effectively recovering 154-173 additional correct answers per 1,034 questions.
40

The repair system’s effectiveness varied significantly by error type, achieving highest success
rates for schema-related errors and syntax mistakes. The system’s ability to provide
targeted hints based on question analysis, combined with complete schema information
and specific error feedback, enabled models to correct many systematic mistakes related
to database structure references.
However, the repair mechanism’s moderate success rate reveals important limitations
in current approaches to automated error correction. The failure to correct 50-57% of
initially wrong queries for strong models, and over 95% for weak models like Qwen Coder,
indicates that many errors involve deeper semantic misunderstandings that cannot be
addressed through additional prompting alone.
The repair mechanism’s severely limited effectiveness on Qwen Coder (4.8% success rate)
demonstrates that repair systems cannot compensate for fundamental deficiencies in SQL
generation capabilities. This finding suggests that repair mechanisms require a baseline
level of model competence to function effectively and cannot serve as universal solutions
for poor text-to-SQL performance.
3.4.3 Implications for Text-to-SQL System Design
These findings provide several important guidelines for text-to-SQL system design and
deployment. First, model selection critically impacts both baseline performance and
repair mechanism effectiveness. Organizations implementing text-to-SQL systems should
prioritize high-capability models like GPT-4o over weaker alternatives, as the performance
differences are substantial and repair mechanisms cannot bridge fundamental capability
gaps.
Second, question preprocessing and user training may be more important than initially
assumed. The question improvement paradox suggests that systems should guide users to-
ward question formulation patterns that align with model training rather than encouraging
more natural language expressions that may actually impair performance.
Third, repair mechanisms provide meaningful value for high-capability models but should
not be expected to solve fundamental model limitations. The 43-49% repair success
rates for strong models justify the implementation complexity, while the poor repair
performance on weak models suggests that repair systems are not viable solutions for
fundamentally inadequate text-to-SQL capabilities.
Finally, the consistent performance patterns across different question types and models
suggest that these findings are likely to generalize to other text-to-SQL applications and
datasets, providing reliable guidance for system design decisions in various organizational
contexts.
